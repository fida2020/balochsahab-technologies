Before an app can be published on Google Play, it needs a privacy policy and a completed Data safety section that tells users what data the app collects and why. Many developers treat these as paperwork to rush through at the end. That is a mistake: inaccurate declarations can lead to rejected updates or enforcement, and they undermine users' trust.

We have filled these in for our own apps, including XnaksView, a short-video and LIVE app with accounts, uploads and in-app messaging. This guide shares the process we follow.

**Please note: this article is general practical guidance, not legal advice.** Privacy laws differ between countries and change over time. For your specific situation, consult a qualified lawyer, and always check Google's current policies in the [Play Console Help Center](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/).

## Step 1: Build a data inventory

You cannot describe your data practices accurately until you know them. Start with a simple document or spreadsheet listing every type of data the app touches. For each item, note:

- **What it is**, for example email address, phone number, precise location, photos and videos, messages, device identifiers or crash logs
- **Where it comes from**: entered by the user, generated by the app, or collected automatically
- **Why you need it**: account management, app functionality, analytics, fraud prevention, advertising and so on
- **Where it goes**: your own servers, and which third-party services
- **Whether it is optional**: can the user use the app without providing it
- **How long you keep it** and how it is deleted

Walk through the app screen by screen: sign-up, profile, uploads, messaging, payments, settings. Then look at the code for permissions in your manifest and for anything sent over the network.

## Step 2: Do not forget third-party SDKs

The most common gap is data collected by libraries rather than your own code. Analytics, crash reporting, advertising, authentication, push notifications, payments, maps and real-time video SDKs may all collect data such as device identifiers, IP addresses or usage information.

Google's position is that you are responsible for declaring data collected by SDKs in your app. For each SDK, read the provider's documentation; many large providers publish a page specifically explaining what to declare in the Data safety form. Note which features of the SDK you actually use, since data collection often depends on configuration. Remove SDKs you no longer need; it is the easiest privacy improvement there is.

## Step 3: Write the privacy policy

A good privacy policy is accurate and readable. It is meant for your users, so write in plain language with clear headings. Typical sections include:

1. **Who you are** and how to contact you, including a company name and an email address
2. **What data you collect**, grouped in a way users understand, such as account data, content you post, and technical data
3. **How you use it**, for each purpose
4. **Who you share it with**, naming categories of service providers and explaining any sharing with other users (for example, public profiles and videos)
5. **How long you keep it**
6. **Users' choices and rights**, including how to access, correct and delete their data
7. **Security measures** in general terms
8. **Children**, including the minimum age for your app
9. **Changes to the policy** and the date of the last update

Google Play requires the policy to be hosted at an active, publicly accessible URL that is not a PDF, and it must also be accessible inside the app. A page on your website is ideal. Avoid copying another company's policy; it will describe their practices, not yours.

## Step 4: Complete the Data safety form

In Play Console, the Data safety section is found under the app content area. It asks a series of questions; your inventory gives you the answers.

### Collected versus shared

These terms have specific meanings in the form. **Collected** generally means data is transmitted off the user's device, to you or to a third party. Data that is processed only on the device is generally not considered collected. **Shared** generally means data is transferred to a third party. Google's guidance lists exceptions, for example transfers to service providers processing data on your behalf, or transfers the user specifically initiates. Read the current definitions in the help center carefully, because they decide many of your answers.

### Data types and purposes

For each data type you collect, the form asks whether it is collected, shared or both, whether collection is required or optional, and for what purposes. Be specific and consistent with your privacy policy.

### Security practices

The form asks whether data is encrypted in transit. If your app uses HTTPS (TLS) for all network traffic, which every modern app should, you can answer yes. Make sure that applies to every connection, including SDK traffic and any older endpoints. Android's default network security configuration blocks unencrypted traffic for apps targeting recent API levels, which helps.

## Step 5: Account deletion

If your app lets users create an account, Google Play requires that users can request deletion of the account and associated data **from within the app**, and also through a **web link** you provide in the Data safety form, so people who have uninstalled the app can still make a request.

A practical setup:

- Add a clearly labelled "Delete account" option in the app's settings, explaining what will be deleted and what may be kept (for example, records you must retain for legal reasons) and for how long.
- Publish a web page that explains how to request deletion, such as a form or an email address, and verify the requester owns the account.
- Make sure deletion actually removes data from your database, storage buckets and backups according to the timeline you state, and from third-party services where applicable.

## Step 6: Keep everything in sync

The privacy policy, the Data safety form and the app's real behaviour must agree. They drift apart easily as the app grows.

- Review the inventory whenever you add a feature, a permission or an SDK.
- Add a privacy check to your release checklist: does this update change what data we collect or share?
- Update the Data safety form before releasing an update that changes data practices.
- Update the "last updated" date on the policy whenever it changes.
- Keep a short internal record of why each declaration was made, which helps when someone asks months later.

Problems Google finds in pre-release testing can also point to undeclared behaviour; our guide to [Google Play pre-launch reports](/blog/google-play-pre-launch-reports.html) explains how to read them.

## Conclusion

A privacy policy and Data safety form are only as good as your understanding of your own app. Build an honest data inventory that includes every SDK, write the policy in plain language, answer the form using Google's definitions of collected and shared, provide in-app and web account deletion, and keep all of it aligned as the app changes. It takes effort the first time, but afterwards it becomes a short review on each release, and your users get a clear, truthful picture of how their data is handled.
